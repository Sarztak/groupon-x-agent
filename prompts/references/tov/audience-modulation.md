# Audience Modulation — Full Reference

> **Source:** B2C TOV v4.6 (April 2026) — Section: "Audience modulation"
> **Cross-reference:** Messaging House v1 — Audience Architecture (see `../messaging-house.md`)

The voice pillars are constant. What flexes is **which pillar leads**, **how value shows up**, **how specific the copy gets**, and **who speaks** (brand or peer). The Messaging House defines four B2C audience segments. This document shows how voice modulates for each.

---

## The Four Segments

### 01 — Salience Play: Spontaneous Locals

**Lead pillar:** Brand Pillar 01 — *Real life, worth leaving for*
**Voice:** Unexpected leads. Curious supports.

**Role of value:** Second. The experience gets the attention; price removes the last hesitation. Never the opening line.

**Voice modulation:** Occasion-led, spontaneous energy. Short, punchy, idea-first. Removes friction; doesn't browse. Lines should feel like a good idea arriving at the right moment.

**Example:** *"A Saturday's coming. Got a plan? We've got thousands."*

When to apply: This is the default voice for brand acquisition, top-of-funnel awareness, paid social acquisition campaigns, and most organic social.

---

### 02 — Growth / Future: Experience Seekers

**Lead pillars:** Brand Pillars 01 + 03 — *Spontaneity + city rediscovery*
**Voice:** Curious leads. Lived supports.

*Includes Gen Z subsegment — see below for distinct guidance.*

**Role of value:** Enabler, not motivator. "Smart spending unlocks more experiences" — never "I used a coupon." Price lowers the barrier to trying; it's never the reason to try.

**Voice modulation:** Discovery-led, specific, insider-toned. Names places. Treats the city as a thing to be known, not searched. Less promotional cadence, more "have you been to this?"

**Example:** *"Third-gen owner. First-gen cannoli."*

When to apply: Editorial SEO, city guides, organic social where the angle is local discovery, CRM to Regulars and Champions, influencer briefs.

---

### 03 — Reactivation: Lapsed Believers

**Lead pillar:** Brand Pillar 02 — *Quality you can actually trust*
**Voice:** Lived leads. Curious supports.

**Role of value:** Familiar hook, not the close. Price re-opens the door because they remember it. Quality proof is what keeps them. Lead with what's changed.

**Voice modulation:** Confident, evidence-rich. Less cheek, more proof. RTBs do real work here — vetted merchants, verified reviews, transparent pricing. Tone says "we've been here, and it's better than you remember."

**Example:** *"Real places. Real reviews. Real prices."*

When to apply: Win-back CRM, retargeting paid social, SEM for branded queries from users who haven't returned, IMP for "we're back" campaigns.

---

### 04 — Retained, Always Served: Value Hunters

**Lead pillars:** Value wedge + Brand Pillar 02 — *Price + quality proof*
**Voice:** All four pillars apply equally.

**Role of value:** First. The hook and the reason. The experience must still deliver — value without quality erodes loyalty fast.

**Voice modulation:** Direct, deliberate, value-clear. Less wordplay, more clarity. The voice is still recognizably Groupon — but the price earns the try, and the quality earns the return.

**Example:** *"50% off, same great spot. Book it before it's gone."*

When to apply: Promotional moments (IMP Cluster A), value-led SEM, retention CRM, last-chance/seasonal copy where price is the news.

## Subsegment: Gen Z (Within Experience Seekers)

Gen Z isn't "younger Experience Seekers." The Messaging House calls this out as a structurally different challenge, and the voice system reflects that. **Three things shift when writing for Gen Z:**

### 1. Brand formation, not salience
Older audiences have a salience gap — they know Groupon but don't think of it first. Gen Z has a **brand formation gap** — many have no strong association at all. Voice should build identity, not refresh memory.

Practically: don't reference "the old Groupon," don't position as "rediscovery." Position as introduction. Lead with the experience, name what Groupon is in plain language, don't assume context.

### 2. Peer voice carries credibility
Brand-authored claims land softly. **UGC, creator content, and real reviews shown in-feed** are the credibility mechanism. Voice rules apply to creative direction and brief language, not to the talent's actual words. Show, don't claim.

Practically: prefer creator-led content over brand-authored ads. When briefing creators, give them the territory and let them write the words. Don't put "third-gen owner" in a creator's mouth — give them the merchant and let them find their own framing.

### 3. Anti-algorithm angle
"Your city has more than you think" becomes **"find it before it's crowded."** Local knowledge as social currency. The offline belief resonates more here than any other segment — screen fatigue is the cultural context, not Groupon's claim. Voice can lean further into offline cues with this audience.

Practically: it's OK to name offline explicitly here. "The phone is the doorway, the experience is the point" can move from subtext to text. Just don't shame the screen — frame the offline as the reward.

## How to Use This in Practice

Before writing, ask:

1. **Which segment is the primary audience for this comm?** (Often defined by the brief or campaign.)
2. **Which pillar leads?** (Look up the segment row above.)
3. **What role does price play?** (Read the "Role of value" line.)
4. **What's the voice modulation?** (Read the voice modulation paragraph.)

Then write — letting the channel guide (`references/channels/`) shape the texture and the format.

If the comm is cross-segment (e.g. broad awareness), default to Spontaneous Locals as the lead segment and Pillar 01 as the lead pillar. That's the default Groupon voice.
