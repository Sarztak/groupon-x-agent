## Headline / Hook Patterns

Every Groupon headline does two jobs: **anchor** (what it is) and **twist** (why it's interesting). The patterns below are different ways to land the twist.

### Pattern A: "X, but Y" (Contrast)

Uses contrast to make the familiar feel new.

- "A pottery class, but messier than expected."
- "Dinner for two, but make it a rooftop."
- "An escape room, but your team is actually the problem."

**Use when:** The category is familiar and this specific experience has a distinctive quality.

---

### Pattern B: Validating Comparison ⭐ THE SIGNATURE PATTERN

Acknowledges what's fine. Proposes something better. **Directly executes "we add, we don't fix."**

- "Your couch is fine. Kayaks are better."
- "Wednesday's a good day. Wednesday with karaoke is a better one."
- "Sunday at home is good. Sunday with dim sum is better."

**Use when:** You'd otherwise be tempted to position the experience as a rescue from boredom. Reach for this pattern instead.

This is the most on-brand pattern Groupon has — flag it as the default move whenever a comparison is in play.

---

### Pattern C: Held-Back Punchline

Sets up the detail. Withholds the payoff. Makes the reader want to know.

- "The pottery studio on 8th St. has a cat. That's all we'll say."
- "There's a speakeasy under a barber shop. Of course there is."
- "This Sunday spot only takes 12 reservations."

**Use when:** Curiosity is doing more work than persuasion — organic social, editorial SEO, top-of-funnel.

---

### Pattern D: Local-Knowledge Flex

Specific street, specific name, specific time. Treats the city as a thing to be known, not searched.

- "Tuesday night jazz, third bar on Main."
- "The Thai place behind the gas station. Trust us on this one."
- "Third-gen owner. First-gen cannoli."

**Use when:** Discovery is the angle — especially for Experience Seekers and Gen Z. Beats "great restaurants near you" every time.

---

### Pattern E: "This should be normal"

Mild cultural observation — positions the experience as something everyone deserves.

- "A 90-minute massage should be the minimum."
- "This is what Tuesdays should feel like."

**Use when:** The experience feels slightly luxurious or unusual, and the goal is to make it feel accessible — without falling into "you deserve it" cliché.

---

### Pattern F: "Probably a good idea"

Understated recommendation. Lets the reader conclude without being pushed.

- "The paddleboarding lesson. Probably worth it."
- "The sushi omakase. Highly recommended by us, specifically."

**Use when:** Value is obvious and copy can afford to be casual.

---

### Pattern G: "You'll survive (and enjoy it)"

Self-aware reassurance. Acknowledges mild anxiety or effort without dismissing it.

- "The intro to rock climbing. You'll survive. You might also get addicted."
- "The improv class. It's awkward for everyone. That's the point."

**Use when:** Experience has a slight barrier — physical challenge, social awkwardness, new skills.

---

### Pattern H: Relatable POV / "When you..."

"POV:" or "When you..." framings, native to social platforms. Drops the viewer into a moment.

- "POV: your friend says 'pick somewhere good' with zero notice"
- "When the brunch place that doesn't take reservations actually takes reservations"

**Use when:** Organic social, especially Register C (creator/influencer). Use sparingly when brand-authored — feels manufactured at scale.

## Humor Mechanisms

Groupon humor is **observational, slightly weird, understated, self-aware**. These are the named mechanisms that produce it.

### Mechanism 1: Contrast

Serious or dry framing applied to a light or absurd subject.

*Formula:* Describe something fun in a very matter-of-fact way.

- "An art class. Wine included. Standards lowered appropriately."
- "Kayaking. Technically exercise. Mostly floating."

---

### Mechanism 2: Understatement

Treat a genuinely good thing as quietly notable rather than loudly amazing.

*Formula:* Say less than the situation seems to call for.

- "A rooftop bar. It is what you think it is."
- "The best ramen in the city, allegedly. (It's definitely the best ramen in the city.)"

---

### Mechanism 3: Literal Interpretation

Take a common phrase or idea at face value, then follow it to a slightly unexpected conclusion.

*Formula:* Identify the implicit promise, then state it plainly.

- "Treat yourself. Not metaphorically — like, actual warm towels."
- "Self-care: still works if you booked it on a deal site."

---

### Mechanism 4: Mild Exaggeration

Push a true observation just past the point of literalism, not into absurdity.

*Formula:* Take a real truth and add one degree of extra.

- "The escape room you'll still be talking about at the all-hands on Monday."
- "A massage so good you'll reconsider how you've been living."

---

### Mechanism 5: Unexpected Framing

Lead with an angle that's slightly off from what the reader expects, then deliver the real offer.

*Formula:* Enter from an oblique direction.

- Not "Book a pottery class" but "Something to do with your hands that isn't scrolling."
- Not "Spa day deals" but "Permission to be unavailable for 90 minutes."

---

## The Five Writing Principles

These come from craft, not the TOV directly, but they're the discipline that produces TOV-quality output:

1. **Lead with value** — The offer must be immediately clear. Personality is decoration on top of understood value.
2. **Cut aggressively** — Read every sentence and ask: does this earn its space? Shorter is almost always better.
3. **One idea per line** — Stacking concepts makes copy feel heavy. One beat. Then move.
4. **Avoid "trying"** — If it feels written to be funny, remove it. Humor that works doesn't announce itself.
5. **Let structure do work** — Spacing, line breaks, sequencing carry clarity. Don't compensate with more words.

---

## The Test for Any Humorous Line

Before keeping a humorous line, ask:

1. **Is it clear?** If someone doesn't get the offer because of the humor, cut it.
2. **Is it earned?** Does the observation apply to this specific thing, or could it apply to anything?
3. **Does it explain itself?** If yes, rewrite or cut.
4. **Does the reader come out looking fine?** ("We add, we don't fix.")
5. **Would the writer be embarrassed if nobody laughed?** Good humor is quiet enough that it lands either way.

---

## Quick Selection Heuristics

- **If the comm is comparing two options →** Pattern B (validating comparison). Default move.
- **If the angle is local discovery →** Pattern D (local-knowledge flex) + Mechanism 5 (unexpected framing).
- **If you want curiosity without persuasion →** Pattern C (held-back punchline).
- **If the experience has a barrier →** Pattern G ("you'll survive").
- **If humor feels tempting →** Default to Mechanism 2 (understatement). Most failures come from going louder, not quieter.
